## Final 5-minute version: "April 3"

Put together by Gadiel and Nico on November 15, focused on product sense (the grounding in docs/04)
and meant for a jury that knows nothing about fire or satellites. It is about 600 words, close to
4:35 with the demo. It needs timing.

**How it is built.** The structure is that of the Pixar story ("Once upon a time… every day… until
one day… because of that… until finally"), and it alternates, as Duarte proposes, between what is
and what could be. The idea that ties everything together is "the memory of fire", taken from
Raskin's option. The hook is a *cold open* (starting in the middle of the scene): a contradiction
that leaves an open question, and that question is closed in the demo. The rules that were
followed:

- One idea and one number per slide (Jobs, Kawasaki).
- The technology is explained with a comparison (binoculars and telescope), like Jobs with "1,000
  songs in your pocket".
- The problem is said in one sentence (Y Combinator).
- The limits are stated before they are asked.
- The close returns to the beginning and asks for something concrete.

**Leo and Nico present, half and half** (about 290 and 275 words). Leo tells the story of his
grandfather, the problem of the countryside, the demo and the close; Nico, who did the
verifications with Gadiel, tells how the satellite works, the solution and the tests. There is one
handoff per slide, never mid-sentence. If one is missing, the other says everything and changes "my
grandfather" to "Leo's grandfather".

**D1 · 0:00–0:12 · Leo** · *Cover (in the background, if possible, the grandfather's photo)*
> *[3 seconds of silence, looking at the jury.]*
> In Muna, Yucatán, the permit to burn closes on April 3. *(pause)* Don Lorenzo, who has been
> planting there for 50 years, says burning starts… in April.

**D2 · 0:12–0:45 · Leo · Once upon a time** · *Grandfather's photo. "'In April'" against "Permit:

*How to say "OF HIS":* slower, stressing "of his", with a short pause before "plot". That emphasis
sets up the sentence that opens D3: "This doesn't happen on a single plot".

**D3 · 0:45–1:35 · Leo, and Nico closes · Every day (the problem)** · *Headline: "Taking care of
fire without knowing what is normal"*
> This doesn't happen on a single plot. In Yucatán, 4 of every 10 hectares are farmland. When a
> burn escapes, the same people lose: in Oxkutzcab, a burn that got out of control destroyed 15
> hectares of lime.
> The one who has to look after that fire is the municipality. The law asks it to authorize each
> burn and, when the season closes, to evaluate it and propose improvements. But today no
> municipality can answer the most basic question: *(pause)* is my season going worse or better
> than normal?
>
> **Nico:** There are systems that see today's fire. None compares it with normal.

**D4 · 1:35–2:45 · Nico · Until one day (why it is hard and how it was done)** · *The chart of the
2012 jump, with binoculars before and telescope after; the three steps, small*
> To know what is normal you need memory, and Yucatán has it: 25 years of NASA satellites watching
> fire every day. Each heat point they see is called a hot spot.
> But that memory is split. Until 2012, the satellite saw as if through binoculars. The new one
> sees as if through a telescope: smaller fires, between three and five times more. If you join
> them as they are, it looks like fire tripled in 2012. *(pause)* It didn't triple. The satellite
> changed.
> Our solution was in three steps.
> One: we took eight years in which both satellites watched Yucatán at the same time, and counted
> how many hot spots the new one sees for each one the old one sees, in each zone and season.
> Two: with that we translated the old years. From 13 years of memory we went to 25.
> Three: we tested it on five years we didn't use. It is off by 11 %; the limit, which we set
> before seeing the result, was 25 %.
> And it is urgent: on November 2, another of those satellites stopped sending data. Each one that
> shuts down adds another piece to the memory.

**D5 · 2:45–3:40 · Leo · Because of that (demo, which closes the hook)** · *Live demo of Muna, with
the backup video ready*
> This is K'áak', "fire" in Maya. I'm going to open Muna. *[Open.]* Here is the answer: of every
> 10 hot spots in a normal year, 8 appear after April 3. *(pause)* My grandfather was right. I'm
> going to tap 2024. *[Tap.]* It is the year with the most hectares burned in Yucatán in the
> decade. Muna had 81 hot spots, almost 1.5 times normal. That is what a season that goes beyond
> normal looks like.
> It all fits in a card that gets forwarded over WhatsApp, and it updates itself four times a day.

**D6 · 3:40–4:20 · Nico · Until finally (tests and ask)** · *Big: "84 %". Below: "Pilot 2027: Muna
and Tekax"*
> And what if the satellite is wrong? We compared it with the wildfires CONAFOR recorded: it saw
> more than half, and 84 % of those over 100 hectares. By chance it would match less than 10 %. It
> doesn't see everything: no hot spots doesn't mean there was no fire. And it doesn't predict
> wildfires: it says what is normal.
> What comes next is going down to each zone of the municipality, to the exact weeks when it burns,
> and testing it in 2027 with Muna and with Tekax, the municipality with the most hectares burned
> in the state.

**D7 · 4:20–4:35 · Leo** · *QR (opens `kaak.pages.dev`, the front page with the search box)*
> My grandfather keeps in his head 50 years of fire of one plot. K'áak' joins 25 years of memory of
> every municipality in Yucatán. Scan the code and look up yours.

### Handoffs

- **D3, from Leo to Nico:** Leo ends with "is my season going worse or better than normal?", pauses
  and turns to look at Nico. Nico comes in with "There are systems that see today's fire…".
- **D4 to D5, from Nico to Leo:** Nico ends with "…another piece to the memory", and Leo, who
  already has the laptop, comes in with "This is K'áak'".
- **D5 to D6, from Leo to Nico:** Leo ends with "…four times a day". Nico comes in with "And what
  if the satellite is wrong?".
- **D6 to D7, from Nico to Leo:** Nico ends with "…Muna and with Tekax". Leo closes.
- Whoever isn't speaking looks at whoever is or at the jury, never at the screen or the phone.

### Who answers in the question round

- **Nico:** everything about the satellite. How it was calibrated, the four tests, what the
  satellite doesn't see, NOAA-20, "three and five", 2022 and Aqua, and the challenge's optional
  parts.
- **Leo:** everything about the countryside and the user. The grandfather, the municipality and the
  law, "will a farmer use this?", the official window and 49 % against 23 %, the pilot and the card.
- Whoever doesn't answer doesn't complete the other's answer, unless asked.

### How the hook is said

- It is the only thing learned word for word. The rest is said with one-word cards per slide
  ("April", "normal", "binoculars", "Muna", "84", "memory").
- Before speaking, 3 seconds of silence standing, looking at the jury. No "Good morning, we are…":
  the team's name is already on the cover.
- It is said slower than the rest, with 2 to 3 seconds of silence after "…in April".
- While the hook is said, don't look at the screen.
- The hook has to close in the demo with "My grandfather was right". If it doesn't close, it is a
  trick.
- Why not open with a question to the audience ("Raise your hand…"): it costs about 15 seconds.
  Before settling on this hook, four others were considered:
  - **"According to the satellites, in 2012 fire tripled. It is false."** It was the one from
    version 2; it puts the satellite first and not the person.
  - **The figure "187 permits against 8,757 hot spots".** It compares different things.
  - **The Yaxkukul smoke.** It was a dump, not a countryside burn.
  - **The question to the audience.** Because of the time it costs.

### Planted doubts for the questions

These are sentences that say something without explaining it, so that the jury asks about what we
best know how to answer. Four at most: if more are left, they look like gaps. To answer: one
sentence of answer, one number, and back to the message ("…that is why the municipality knows what
is normal").

**Questions it is better not to provoke:** whether K'áak' would have seen the Yaxkukul fire, why
Aqua saw less fire in 2022, or whether fire is increasing. None is touched in this version. If they
are asked, the answers are in "Likely jury questions" and in "What is not said".

**The question that is sure to come: "Will a farmer use this?"** "No, and the grandfather told us:
'they don't adapt to technology'. That is why the user is the municipality, and the town gets a
card over WhatsApp, not a website."

**If they ask about "between three and five"** (the page, in season, shows 2.8 to 3.8): "It depends
on the zone and the season: in burning season it is 3 to 4; out of season, in the east, it reaches
5." "Three and five" stays because that is what was said in the video.

**If they ask about permits:** "In 2023 there were 187 permits and the satellite saw 8,757 hot
spots. Not every hot spot is a burn, but the difference is enormous." It doesn't go in the pitch
because it compares different things.

### What changed from the earlier 5-minute version, and why

- **The hook.** Before there were 40 seconds of story before reaching the problem. Now the
  contradiction between "April 3" and "in April" states the problem in 12 seconds, and the demo
  closes it.
- **The problem is said in one sentence:** "is my season going worse or better than normal?".
- **How it was done:** three steps and the binoculars comparison, instead of satellite names.
- **The calibration test (11 %, with the limit set beforehand)** enters the pitch. Before there was
  only the CONAFOR one, which proves the satellite sees fire, not that the translation works.
- **Removed:**
  - the Yaxkukul smoke: it cost a minute and it was a dump;
  - the hectares year by year;
  - the 19 % inside the window: the 49 % against 23 % is kept for the questions;
  - "1 permit for every 47 hot spots".
- **The close asks for something concrete:** the 2027 pilot with Muna and Tekax.
- **From option B (Raskin) were taken** the memory idea, the definition of hot spot and the close.
  **Not taken** was "the memory shuts down": the data stay archived in FIRMS and NOAA-20 continues,
  so a judge can refute it. The memory is split, not shut down.

**[0:00–0:35] The grandfather**
This is my grandfather Jesús. He has been planting his plot in Muna, Yucatán, for more than 50
years. If you ask him when the fire hot spots start, he doesn't think about it: "in April." He has
never seen a chart, but he has a fire calendar in his head.

And he learned that calendar by burning and planting, year after year, not because someone showed
him how each season is going.

But that calendar belongs to a single plot.

**[0:35–1:10] The problem, at the scale of the state**
Yucatán has 106 municipalities. Every year, the satellite sees between 6,800 and 14,500 fire hot
spots. Forest statistics record dozens of wildfires. 89 % of that fire falls between March and
May, and my grandfather is right: the peak is April.

Whoever has to prepare the state can't have the grandfather's calendar for every municipality. We
reviewed CONABIO's alert system: it sees the fire, but each satellite goes separately and it
doesn't compare a season against normal.

**[1:10–1:45] Why join the satellites**
To know what is "normal" you need a long history, and that history is in two satellites that don't
speak the same language. VIIRS sees between three and five times more fire than MODIS. If you glue
them together uncalibrated, it looks like fire tripled in 2012. It didn't triple: the sensor
changed. With VIIRS alone there are only 13 years. Calibrated together, there are 25.

**[1:45–2:15] Demo**
This is K'áak', "fire" in Maya. We pick Muna, where my grandfather plants. In a normal year it has
56 hot spots from March to May. In 2026 it had 24: less than half, its season with the least fire
since 2012. And there too fire arrives in April: 8 of every 10 hot spots appear after April 3.
*[Search Muna. Say what the screen shows that day.]* And the card fits in a WhatsApp message: the
commissioner forwards it to the town.

**[2:15–2:40] Tests**
How do we know it works? Of the wildfires CONAFOR recorded in the peninsula, the satellite saw
more than half, and 84 % of those over 100 hectares. By chance it would match less than 10 %. And
we crossed it with the official burning calendar: in 2025, only 19 % of the fire fell inside its

**[2:40–3:00] For whom and close**

which receive their municipality's card. It doesn't predict wildfires or send alerts: it says

My grandfather Jesús has spent 50 years learning the fire calendar of one plot. We give it to the
whole state.

## Countryside block (proposal for the 3-minute version)

It goes between "The grandfather" and "The problem, at the scale of the state". It is about 50
seconds: to avoid running over 3 minutes, say only the first and last sentence of each paragraph
(about 25 seconds). The 3-minute version already runs about 2:55: when timing (#9) we have to decide
what comes out. Figures in docs/01, "Yucatán's countryside", "The municipalities with the most fire
live off the land" and "Each zone's calendar captures double the official window". Detail in
docs/04.

**What the countryside is.** In Yucatán, 4 of every 10 hectares are farmland. More than 115
thousand people work there full time, and more than 9 in 10 producers consider themselves
indigenous. That land produces the raw material of what we eat: the maize for tortillas, the
cattle's pasture, milk, citrus and meat, which is the second product Yucatán sells abroad the most.

**Where the fire is.** We reviewed the 10 municipalities with the most fire in the state. All 10
live off the land: the cattle-ranching east, like Tizimín, Panabá and Buctzotz, and the milpa
south, like Tekax, Yaxcabá and Peto. None is a city like Mérida. Those 10 municipalities hold
almost 6 of every 10 wildfires in the state.

**Why prevent.** To plant, people burn: it is their work tool. But when fire escapes, the ones who
lose are the same people who work the land. In Oxkutzcab, a brush burn that got out of control
destroyed 15 hectares of Persian lime. And these are people with nothing to recover with: 8 in 10
hectares depend on rain, and only 2 in 100 production units got credit.

**Conclusion.** Preventing a wildfire in Yucatán is not only caring for the forest. It is caring
for the work and the food of the people of the countryside, the vast majority of whom consider
themselves indigenous, and for the raw material of the state's whole food chain.

**The calendar, in one sentence** (for "Tests" or the close): "In the zones that burn every year,
only 1 in 4 fires falls inside the official window; half fall in 4 weeks that repeat year after
year. That helps SEDER and the municipalities plan."

## Likely jury questions

Short answers, with their source in docs/01.

- **"In the validation, why does 2022 come out worse than the other years?"**
  "Aqua, one of the two old satellites, was off for 17 days in the middle of the 2022 season
  because of a failure NASA reported. Those days are not counted. Also, that year Aqua saw less
  fire than normal, and its twin Terra didn't, so it is something about the satellite and not about
  the fire. Even so the test passes: 11 % error over five years that were not used to calibrate."
  Source: "2022: Aqua was off for 17 days and saw less fire than normal".

- **"The challenge asks to choose dates, draw an area and download the hot spots. Why isn't that
  there?"**
  "They are the optional parts of the challenge and we left them for later on purpose, but each one
  has something in its place. Instead of drawing an area, you pick your municipality or tap 'Use my
  location', which turns your latitude and longitude into your municipality. Instead of a free date
  range, you see the March to May season of any year since 2001, day by day, against normal. And
  there are three tables to download: the hot spots of each day of your municipality, the burning
  calendar by month and the harmonized series for the whole peninsula, by week, since 2001. Next is
  typing coordinates by hand and drawing an area."
  If they ask why it wasn't done: for a drawn area you have to add up the 1 km cells that fall
  inside it, and the site stores the data already added up by municipality. Typing coordinates is
  cheaper: "Use my location" already does that calculation.
  Source: the site. Municipality ("Download the data for … (CSV table)" and the year strip), Dates
  ("Download the table (CSV)") and Method (`serie_armonizada.csv`).

- **"Why is the calendar day by day and not by week or by month?"**
  "Because SEDER gives burning permits by date: in zone 2, from April 20 to May 31. By week or by
  month, that limit would fall in the middle of a block and we could no longer tell the
  commissioner how many hot spots appear after the date. The normal year is not noisy: it is the
  average shape of 25 seasons. And if you want to see the coarse pattern, the almanac goes by month
  and the series you download goes by week."
  Source: the site (Municipality, Dates and Method) and docs/01, "A normal year, day by day".
  Decision of #40, made after the challenge author's answer in the official forum
  (https://github.com/nasa/spaceapps/discussions/1879).

## What is not said

- That fire is increasing: 2025 and 2026 were the lowest seasons on record.
- "Illegal burns": a hot spot outside its window can have other causes.
- That K'áak' predicts wildfires, that it is an official alert system or that the satellite sees all
  the fire.
- That agriculture is the second sector of Yucatán's economy, or "63 % of the food economy": there
  is no source. What is true: beef is the second product Yucatán exports the most.
- "Half a million agricultural hectares" (it is 300 to 436 thousand) or that farmland "fell" from
  2022 to 2025 (the census and the survey measure differently).
- That the challenge asks for a calendar: we couldn't open the challenge page to confirm it.
- That SEDER's window "is wrong" or "hits": it says when burning is allowed, not when it is
  burned. Also not "25 years" for each zone's calendar: it is 14 years of VIIRS.
- Nothing about whether Civil Protection informs people or not: it is not in docs/01 and it is a
  main user of the project.
- About the Yaxkukul smoke: that it spread to other municipalities in the north or that it affected
  people's health. The notes only talk about Mérida and report no symptoms or air-quality figures.
  Also not that it was a wildfire or an agricultural burn: it was the municipal dump.
- About 2022: why Aqua saw less fire that year (it isn't confirmed), or that it was due to its orbit
  drift (the data doesn't support it). What can be said is the 17-day outage.
- That the site already lets you choose any date range, draw an area or type coordinates: it
  doesn't (#36).
- That "the memory of fire shuts down": the data stay archived in FIRMS and NOAA-20 continues. What
  is true: "it is split" and "each satellite that shuts down adds another piece".
- "My grandfather", if the one telling it is not Leo: say "Leo's grandfather".
- "1 permit for every 47 hot spots" as proof of the problem: a hot spot is not a burn.
- About cattle ranching (#60):
  - that it is the state's second source of income: it is false (the primary sector is 3.7 % of
    Yucatán's GDP);
  - any peso amount of losses from wildfires: there is no public estimate;
  - that hot spots over pasture are cattle or illegal burns: the satellite doesn't see the cause;
  - Tizimín's herd according to Ugroy (190 thousand or 110 thousand head): the figures clash. What
    is true: INEGI's census gives 139,472 in 2022.
