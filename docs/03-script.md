## 3-minute version

About 425 words; at pitch pace (140–150 per minute) that is close to 2:55. It needs timing with
the real demo.

**[0:00–0:35] The grandfather**
This is my grandfather Jesús. He has been planting his plot in Muna, Yucatán, for more than 50
years. If you ask him when the fire hot spots start, he doesn't think about it: "in April." He has
never seen a chart, but he has a fire calendar in his head.

And he learned that calendar by burning and planting, year after year, not because someone showed
him how each season is going.

But that calendar belongs to a single plot.

**[0:35–1:10] The problem, at the scale of the state**
Yucatán has 106 municipalities. Every year, the satellite sees between 6,800 and 14,500 fire hot
spots. Forest statistics record dozens of wildfires. 89 % of that fire falls between March and
May, and my grandfather is right: the peak is April.

Whoever has to prepare the state can't have the grandfather's calendar for every municipality. We
reviewed CONABIO's alert system: it sees the fire, but each satellite goes separately and it
doesn't compare a season against normal.

**[1:10–1:45] Why join the satellites**
To know what is "normal" you need a long history, and that history is in two satellites that don't
speak the same language. VIIRS sees between three and five times more fire than MODIS. If you glue
them together uncalibrated, it looks like fire tripled in 2012. It didn't triple: the sensor
changed. With VIIRS alone there are only 13 years. Calibrated together, there are 25.

**[1:45–2:15] Demo**
This is K'áak', "fire" in Maya. We pick Umán. In a normal year it has 42 hot spots from March to
May. In 2025, the second quietest year in the state, it had 63: one and a half times normal.
*[Tap 2025 in its bars. Say what the screen shows that day.]* And the card fits in a WhatsApp
message: the commissioner forwards it to the town.

**[2:15–2:40] Tests**
How do we know it works? Of the wildfires CONAFOR recorded in the peninsula, the satellite saw
more than half, and 84 % of those over 100 hectares. By chance it would match less than 10 %. And
we crossed it with the official burning calendar: in 2025, only 19 % of the fire fell inside its
zone's window. It is not an accusation, it is a question for SEDER and Civil Protection.

**[2:40–3:00] For whom and close**
K'áak' is for SEDER and Civil Protection, who decide where and when to prepare, and for the towns,
which receive their municipality's card. It doesn't predict wildfires or send alerts: it says
whether the season goes beyond normal, upward or downward.

My grandfather Jesús has spent 50 years learning the fire calendar of one plot. We give it to the
whole state.

## 5-minute version

It starts from the 3-minute one and adds the Yaxkukul and Mérida smoke, the variation from one year
to the next, the urgency of the satellites and the Umán example with what CONAFOR recorded. It is
about 720 words, close to 4:55 at pitch pace not counting the demo. It needs timing.

**[0:00–0:40] The grandfather**
This is my grandfather Jesús. He has been planting his plot in Muna, Yucatán, for more than 50
years. If you ask him when the fire hot spots start, he doesn't think about it: "in April." He has
never seen a chart, but he has a fire calendar in his head.

And he learned that calendar by burning and planting, year after year, not because someone showed
him how each season is going. But that calendar belongs to a single plot.

**[0:40–1:35] Smoke doesn't stay where it starts**
A few days ago, on the night of Monday, November 9, the Yaxkukul dump caught fire. It is 26
kilometers from Mérida. That night, the smoke reached neighborhoods in the north, the center and
the west. Many people thought it was fog. Civil Protection had to ask people to stay home, with
doors and windows closed, and to look after children, older adults and pregnant women.

A dump is not a milpa. But it shows something we almost never see: a single fire, far away, and
the smoke reaches people who never saw it start. In May 2024, Mérida was among the five most
polluted cities in Mexico, with "very bad" air, because of smoke from fires in the days before.

Fire seems far away, from the bush, from another municipality. Even if it doesn't seem so, it
affects us.

**[1:35–2:20] The problem, at the scale of the state**
Yucatán has 106 municipalities. Every year, the satellite sees between 6,800 and 14,500 fire hot
spots. Forest statistics record dozens of wildfires. 89 % falls between March and May, and my
grandfather is right: the peak is April.

But the grandfather's calendar says when, not whether this season is going worse or better than
normal. And that changes enormously: in Yucatán 765 hectares burned in 2023, 20,334 in 2024, the
worst year in the series, and 2,215 in 2025.

We reviewed CONABIO's alert system: it sees the fire, but each satellite goes separately and it
doesn't compare a season against normal.

**[2:20–3:00] Why join the satellites**
To know what is "normal" you need a long history, and that history is in satellites that don't
speak the same language. VIIRS sees between three and five times more fire than MODIS. If you glue
them together uncalibrated, it looks like fire tripled in 2012. It didn't triple: the sensor
changed. With VIIRS alone there are only 13 years. Calibrated together, there are 25.

And it is urgent: MODIS is being retired and S-NPP stopped sending data on November 2. The 25 years
are split among four satellites.

**[3:00–3:40] Demo**
This is K'áak', "fire" in Maya. We pick Umán. In a normal year it has 42 hot spots from March to
May. In 2025, the second quietest year in the state, it had 63: one and a half times normal. And it
isn't only the satellite that says so: according to preliminary CONAFOR figures, that year 264
hectares of jungle burned in Umán, and in March a fire reached next to two housing developments.
*[Tap 2025 in its bars. Say what the screen shows that day.]* And the card fits in a WhatsApp
message: the commissioner forwards it to the town.

**[3:40–4:25] Tests**
How do we know it works? Of the wildfires CONAFOR recorded in the peninsula, the satellite saw more
than half, and 84 % of those over 100 hectares. By chance it would match less than 10 %: that is
about six times chance. The bigger the wildfire, the more likely it sees it.

And we crossed it with the official burning calendar: in 2025, only 19 % of the fire fell inside

we are clear about the limit: no hot spots doesn't mean there was no fire.

**[4:25–5:00] For whom and close**
K'áak' is for SEDER and Civil Protection, who decide where and when to prepare, and for the towns,

It doesn't predict wildfires or send alerts: it says whether the season goes beyond normal, upward
or downward.

## Countryside block (proposal for the 3-minute version)

It goes between "The grandfather" and "The problem, at the scale of the state". It is about 50
seconds: to avoid running over 3 minutes, say only the first and last sentence of each paragraph
(about 25 seconds). The 3-minute version already runs about 2:55: when timing (#9) we have to decide
what comes out. Figures in docs/01, "Yucatán's countryside", "The municipalities with the most fire
live off the land" and "Each zone's calendar captures double the official window". Detail in
docs/04.

**What the countryside is.** In Yucatán, 4 of every 10 hectares are farmland. More than 115
thousand people work there full time, and more than 9 in 10 producers consider themselves
indigenous. That land produces the raw material of what we eat: the maize for tortillas, the
cattle's pasture, milk, citrus and meat, which is the second product Yucatán sells abroad the most.

**Where the fire is.** We reviewed the 10 municipalities with the most fire in the state. All 10
live off the land: the cattle-ranching east, like Tizimín, Panabá and Buctzotz, and the milpa
south, like Tekax, Yaxcabá and Peto. None is a city like Mérida. Those 10 municipalities hold
almost 6 of every 10 wildfires in the state.

**Why prevent.** To plant, people burn: it is their work tool. But when fire escapes, the ones who
lose are the same people who work the land. In Oxkutzcab, a brush burn that got out of control
destroyed 15 hectares of Persian lime. And these are people with nothing to recover with: 8 in 10
hectares depend on rain, and only 2 in 100 production units got credit.

**Conclusion.** Preventing a wildfire in Yucatán is not only caring for the forest. It is caring
for the work and the food of the people of the countryside, the vast majority of whom consider
themselves indigenous, and for the raw material of the state's whole food chain.

**The calendar, in one sentence** (for "Tests" or the close): "In the zones that burn every year,
only 1 in 4 fires falls inside the official window; half fall in 4 weeks that repeat year after
year. That helps SEDER and the municipalities plan."

## Likely jury questions

Short answers, with their source in docs/01.

- **"In the validation, why does 2022 come out worse than the other years?"**
  "Aqua, one of the two old satellites, was off for 17 days in the middle of the 2022 season
  because of a failure NASA reported. Those days are not counted. Also, that year Aqua saw less
  fire than normal, and its twin Terra didn't, so it is something about the satellite and not about
  the fire. Even so the test passes: 11 % error over five years that were not used to calibrate."
  Source: "2022: Aqua was off for 17 days and saw less fire than normal".

- **"The challenge asks to choose dates, draw an area and download the hot spots. Why isn't that
  there?"**
  "They are the optional parts of the challenge and we left them for later on purpose, but each one
  has something in its place. Instead of drawing an area, you pick your municipality or tap 'Use my
  location', which turns your latitude and longitude into your municipality. Instead of a free date
  range, you see the March to May season of any year since 2001, day by day, against normal. And
  there are three tables to download: the hot spots of each day of your municipality, the burning
  calendar by month and the harmonized series for the whole peninsula, by week, since 2001. Next is
  typing coordinates by hand and drawing an area."
  If they ask why it wasn't done: for a drawn area you have to add up the 1 km cells that fall
  inside it, and the site stores the data already added up by municipality. Typing coordinates is
  cheaper: "Use my location" already does that calculation.
  Source: the site. Municipality ("Download the data for … (CSV table)" and the year strip), Dates
  ("Download the table (CSV)") and Method (`serie_armonizada.csv`).

- **"Why is the calendar day by day and not by week or by month?"**
  "Because SEDER gives burning permits by date: in zone 2, from April 20 to May 31. By week or by
  month, that limit would fall in the middle of a block and we could no longer tell the
  commissioner how many hot spots appear after the date. The normal year is not noisy: it is the
  average shape of 25 seasons. And if you want to see the coarse pattern, the almanac goes by month
  and the series you download goes by week."
  Source: the site (Municipality, Dates and Method) and docs/01, "A normal year, day by day".
  Decision of #40, made after the challenge author's answer in the official forum
  (https://github.com/nasa/spaceapps/discussions/1879).

## What is not said

- That fire is increasing: 2025 and 2026 were the lowest seasons on record.
- "Illegal burns": a hot spot outside its window can have other causes.
- That K'áak' predicts wildfires, that it is an official alert system or that the satellite sees all
  the fire.
- That agriculture is the second sector of Yucatán's economy, or "63 % of the food economy": there
  is no source. What is true: beef is the second product Yucatán exports the most.
- "Half a million agricultural hectares" (it is 300 to 436 thousand) or that farmland "fell" from
  2022 to 2025 (the census and the survey measure differently).
- That the challenge asks for a calendar: we couldn't open the challenge page to confirm it.
- That SEDER's window "is wrong" or "hits": it says when burning is allowed, not when it is
  burned. Also not "25 years" for each zone's calendar: it is 14 years of VIIRS.
- Nothing about whether Civil Protection informs people or not: it is not in docs/01 and it is a
  main user of the project.
- About the Yaxkukul smoke: that it spread to other municipalities in the north or that it affected
  people's health. The notes only talk about Mérida and report no symptoms or air-quality figures.
  Also not that it was a wildfire or an agricultural burn: it was the municipal dump.
- About 2022: why Aqua saw less fire that year (it isn't confirmed), or that it was due to its orbit
  drift (the data doesn't support it). What can be said is the 17-day outage.
- That the site already lets you choose any date range, draw an area or type coordinates: it
  doesn't (#36).
